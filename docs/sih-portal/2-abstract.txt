THE PROBLEM

Every weighing instrument sold in India, from a kirana counter scale and a jeweller's balance to a 40-tonne weighbridge, must first receive model approval from the Department of Consumer Affairs (Legal Metrology Act, 2009, s.22). Without that certificate no State can issue a manufacturing or import licence (LM (Approval of Models) Rules, 2011, Rule 20). The certificate rests on a type-evaluation test report: about 17 tests under OIML R 76, recorded in the roughly 62-page format of OIML R 76-2, or under India's Seventh Schedule, Heading A of the LM (General) Rules, 2011. NAWIs are the largest single segment of approvals: 11 of the 16 public 2026 certificates we sampled from the national register were NAWIs.

The application side is already digital on eMaap. From the moment a model reaches a Regional Reference Standards Laboratory (RRSL), however, the report is still built by hand in spreadsheets and document templates, and the Assistant/Deputy Director then re-checks the arithmetic by hand. The problem statement names the result: "time-consuming, prone to calculation errors and lacking uniformity." After model-approval services went online, end-to-end time fell from 6–9 months to 3–4 months (DoCA Annual Report 2022-23). What remains inside those months is testing and the paper report chain.

The errors are not careless; they are structural. Each cell is computed: error E = I + ½e − ΔL − L, corrected error Ec = E − E₀, pass if |Ec| ≤ mpe. The maximum permissible error (mpe) depends on the accuracy class and on the load expressed in verification intervals e. On a multi-interval instrument, e itself changes with the load. In R 76-1's own worked example (Max 2/5/15 kg, e = 1/2/10 g, class III), the limit at 4,000 g is ±2 g but at 4,200 g it is ±3 g, in the same partial range and with nothing changing on the display. A spreadsheet that uses one e for the whole instrument gets every row past the first changeover wrong. Add the rounding rule (only when d > 0.2e), in-service limits at twice the initial ones, time-based tests (creep, zero return, temperature drift), disturbance tests, and applicability rules that differ by class and construction, and every officer is re-deriving the standard for every model. A disapproved model costs the manufacturer a fresh application and fresh fees (Rule 10); the testing fee for a digital model is ₹25,000.

OUR SOLUTION: TARAAZU

Taraazu (तराज़ू, the Hindi word for a balance) is a web and offline-capable application built on one idea: we do not fill the report, we compute it, and the 62-page form is only a rendering.

1. A deterministic calculation engine. All R 76-2 forms reduce to six calculation kernels: error against mpe; discrimination; sensitivity; time-based checks (creep, zero return, temperature effect, span stability); significant fault under electrical disturbance; and stability of equilibrium. A shared, multi-interval-aware resolver first finds the partial range a load falls in, then the band, in the order the standard prescribes. It reproduces every step of the standard's worked example (±0.5 → ±1 → ±2 → ±3 → ±10 g). The engine is pure functions: the same inputs always give the same verdict, which is what makes it fully testable and reproducible.

2. The standard is data, not code. Accuracy classes, mpe bands, applicability rules, form layouts and clause citations live in versioned, schema-validated rule packs. Three ship today: OIML R 76 (2006/2007), India's Seventh Schedule Heading A (checked line by line against the legal text, including India's class II* and its mixed use of "IV" and "IIII" for the ordinary class), and the OIML committee draft that restructures R 76 from two parts into five. Rule 7(1)(a) permits conformity to either the Indian rules or OIML, so the officer chooses per report. Each report records the pack version it was evaluated under, so a later update can never change an issued result. When OIML publishes its next edition, only the pack changes.

3. Automatic test planning. From the instrument profile, Taraazu decides which forms apply. Each inapplicable form is printed "– –" together with the clause that excuses it (for example, sensitivity applies only to non-self-indicating instruments), so no two laboratories can disagree on scope.

4. Validation that catches what people miss. The profile is checked against the classification tables (e must be 1, 2 or 5 × 10^k; n and Min within class limits; partial ranges consistent). Software identification is checked as R 76-1 Annex G requires. In the public register we found this field filled with file sizes, moduli, placeholder text and identical values across different manufacturers; Taraazu flags each of these on entry.

5. Two solvers unique to Taraazu. The family/EUT solver (R 76-1 §3.10.4) finds the minimum set of variants to test physically. It reproduces R 76-1 Table 8 exactly (8 variants, only 3 tested) and writes the certificate's own "shall also cover…" clause, which appears in 12 of 16 real certificates we sampled. The Annex F checker confirms that a separately certified indicator and load cell are compatible before testing begins.

6. An evidence-grade record. Every observation, correction, checklist answer and decision is an entry in an append-only log, linked by a SHA-256 hash chain and signed by each officer with Ed25519, as ISO/IEC 17025 §7.8 requires; nothing is edited in place. The approval chain mirrors the Rules: Metrological Assistant → Assistant/Deputy Director → Director (Legal Metrology), including deficiency returns, the Director's remit-back (Rule 8) and the provisional-certificate path. Role-based access means no single account can take a report from bench to certificate. The IT Act, 2000 §§4–5 give such electronic records and signatures legal standing.

7. One dataset, three outputs. A bilingual (Hindi + English) PDF faithful to R 76-2 is the record copy. An editable DOCX, as the problem statement asks, is watermarked DRAFT and bound to the PDF's hash so it can never pass as the record. A machine-readable JSON, modelled on PTB's Digital Calibration Certificate, carries the same data. All three come from one assembled document, so they cannot disagree.

8. Repository and dashboard. Search by manufacturer, model, class, status and date; instrument-wise history; in-process, completed and deficiency counts; and the annual return that Rule 4(9) already requires of every approved laboratory (models tested, fees realised, time taken per test), computed from the log rather than re-typed.

There is deliberately no AI in the legally relevant path. OIML's position is that AI is not yet "court-proof" for legal metrology, so every verdict in Taraazu is deterministic, explainable and traceable to a clause.

FEASIBILITY

Taraazu is a working prototype, not a mock-up, live at https://taraazu.vercel.app. It covers 36 R 76-2 form pages, 34 test rules and a 363-item checklist (17.1–17.4). It passes 516 automated tests, including golden tests written from the standards' own worked examples before the code existed (the multi-interval walk, Table 8, Annex F.6.1). Every threshold is logged against the page of the standard it came from. The stack is TypeScript, React, Node.js/Fastify, SQLite (PostgreSQL for central deployment) and Playwright for PDF. It runs in a browser or offline on one lab PC. The deployment path fits the government stack: NIC/MeghRaj hosting, NIC SSO and eSign/DSC, a CERT-In audit, GIGW 3.0 accessibility, and confidentiality tiers for manufacturers' drawings (Rule 16(2)).

IMPACT

For RRSL officers, the arithmetic and hand re-checking disappear while their judgement stays central; review becomes reading a computed report with a full audit trail. For manufacturers and MSMEs: faster certificates, fewer rejections caused by calculation errors, fewer physical tests for model families, and reports fit for India's role since 2023 as an OIML certificate-issuing authority. For consumers, the gate every weighing instrument passes through becomes faster, uniform and auditable. For the Department: one yardstick across all six RRSLs and the Government Approved Test Centres, and India's first structured, machine-readable dataset of NAWI test results.

ROADMAP

Pilot at one RRSL with NIC SSO and eSign, measuring time-to-report before and after; integration with eMaap and the National Single Window System; GATC tenancy; an in-service mode (mpe × 2) for State-level re-verification; and new rule packs for other OIML recommendations the Department approves models under.
